UNIT 01 FOUNDATIONS

An Introduction to Security and Risk Management

Security involves protecting people, information, computer systems and organizational assets from threats that may cause harm. Risk management is the process of identifying, analyzing, evaluating and treating risks that could prevent an organization from achieving its objectives. It helps organizations understand the problems they may face and make informed decisions about protecting their resources. Effective security and risk management reduce the likelihood of security incidents, financial losses and disruptions to business operations.

The risk management process involves identifying risks, assessing their likelihood and impact, selecting appropriate responses, implementing controls and monitoring their effectiveness. Risks can be treated by avoiding them, reducing their impact, transferring them or accepting them when they fall within an acceptable level. Qualitative assessment uses descriptions such as low, medium and high, while quantitative assessment uses numerical data and financial estimates. Combining these approaches helps organizations prioritize risks and select appropriate security measures.

Key takeawayRisk management is continuous: identify, assess, treat, communicate and review.
UNIT 02 PEOPLE & PROCESS

Users, Assessments and the Risk Management Process

Qualitative and quantitative assessments are important methods used to evaluate risks. Qualitative assessment relies on descriptions and risk-rating categories, making it useful when detailed numerical information is unavailable. Quantitative assessment uses numbers, probabilities and estimated financial losses to measure potential consequences. Although quantitative methods can provide measurable results, they require reliable data and may be more complex to perform. Organizations can use both methods depending on their needs, resources and the information available.

User participation improves risk management because users understand how systems and procedures operate in practice. They can identify weaknesses and challenges that technical teams may overlook. Involving users also encourages cooperation when new security measures are introduced. Recommended mitigations may involve costs, training and changes to existing procedures. For example, multi-factor authentication improves account protection but may add extra login steps. Organizations should consider the benefits, costs and effects of security controls before implementing them.

Key takeawayInvolve the people who use a system when assessing risks and designing controls.
UNIT 03 THREAT ANALYSIS

Introduction to Threat Modelling and Management

Threat modelling is a structured process used to identify potential attacks against a system and determine how to prevent or reduce them. A threat is a possible event or action that could cause harm, while a vulnerability is a weakness that a threat may exploit. For example, a phishing attack is a threat, while weak security awareness among employees may create a vulnerability. Understanding this difference helps organizations identify security problems and develop suitable controls before incidents occur.

Several techniques support threat modelling. STRIDE identifies spoofing, tampering, repudiation, information disclosure, denial of service and elevation of privilege. DREAD provides a method for rating threats according to damage, reproducibility, exploitability, affected users and discoverability, although its scoring can be subjective. Attack trees illustrate different ways an attacker might achieve an objective, while PASTA connects technical threats with business risks. These techniques help security teams examine systems systematically and prioritize important threats.

Key takeawayThreat modelling helps teams think about how a system could be attacked before it is deployed.
UNIT 04 APPLICATION

Application of Threat Modelling and Management Techniques

Threat modelling techniques help organizations identify weaknesses during system design, development and maintenance. The Threat Modeling Manifesto encourages practical, collaborative and risk-focused approaches that help teams understand potential attacks and make better security decisions. OWASP resources provide guidance for identifying application security weaknesses and selecting suitable controls. Applying these resources during development allows teams to address problems such as insecure authentication, unauthorized access and unsafe handling of sensitive information before they become serious issues.

MITRE ATT&CK supports threat management by documenting real-world adversary tactics and techniques. Security teams can use this knowledge to understand attacker behaviour, identify gaps in existing defenses and improve threat detection and incident response. When applying threat modelling to a practical system, teams should identify important assets, users, data flows and trust boundaries before assessing threats and recommending controls. The model should be reviewed whenever systems or threats change so that security measures remain effective.

Key takeawayUse recognized frameworks as practical aids, then tailor the results to the system and its risks.
UNIT 05 STANDARDS

Introduction to Security and Risk Standards in Industry and Enterprise

Security and risk management standards provide recognized requirements and guidelines for protecting information and managing organizational risks. They help organizations establish consistent procedures, evaluate security performance and demonstrate that appropriate controls are being implemented. ISO/IEC 27001 supports the establishment and maintenance of an information security management system, while the NIST Cybersecurity Framework helps organizations understand and manage cybersecurity risks. PCI DSS provides security requirements for organizations that handle payment card data within its scope.

Standards and regulations influence how organizations assess risks, protect information and maintain compliance. Organizations should identify the requirements relevant to their industries, locations, customers and data-processing activities. Applicable data-protection laws may require safeguards for personal information, while payment card requirements address the protection of cardholder data. Failure to meet relevant obligations can lead to financial penalties, reputational damage, security incidents and loss of customer trust. Regular reviews help organizations maintain appropriate practices as their operations change.

Key takeawayChoose standards and legal requirements that apply to the organization's context and obligations.
UNIT 06 IMPLEMENTATION

Practical Implications of Security and Risk Standards

Applying security standards requires organizations to translate requirements into practical controls. The General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR), where applicable, establishes obligations concerning personal data protection and individuals' rights. Organizations may need to limit data collection, restrict access, secure personal information and establish procedures for managing data breaches. They should assess whether the regulation applies to their activities and implement the relevant requirements. These measures support responsible information handling and help maintain public trust.

PCI DSS is designed to protect payment card data through requirements involving access control, secure systems, monitoring and protection of cardholder information. Other standards may guide organizations in establishing security policies, managing incidents and assessing risks. Although implementing standards can require money, training, documentation and technical improvements, these efforts can reduce vulnerabilities and improve security. Organizations should prioritize controls according to their risks, verify that the controls work effectively and review them regularly as threats and requirements evolve.

Key takeawayStandards become valuable when their requirements are translated into working controls and reviewed.
UNIT 07 ASSESSMENT

Risk Management and Security Assessment

Security assessment and risk management work together to protect organizational assets. Security assessments identify weaknesses, threats and gaps in existing controls, while risk management determines which findings require attention and how they should be addressed. This information enables organizations to understand their exposure and make informed decisions about security improvements. A risk assessment may consider the likelihood of an incident, the severity of its consequences, the importance of affected assets and the effectiveness of current safeguards.

Prioritizing risks is important because organizations have limited time, money and personnel. Risks with a high likelihood of occurrence and serious consequences may require immediate action, while lower-priority risks can be monitored or addressed later. Assessment findings can lead to measures such as stronger authentication, improved access permissions, employee training and better monitoring. Regular reassessment is necessary because new technologies, business processes and emerging threats can change an organization's risk exposure.

Key takeawayUse assessment findings to prioritize improvements, then reassess as conditions change.
UNIT 08 QUANTITATIVE RISK

Implementing Quantitative Risk Models

Quantitative risk modelling uses numerical information to estimate the likelihood and impact of risks. It helps organizations compare alternatives, estimate potential financial losses and determine how to allocate resources. Monte Carlo simulation is one technique that repeatedly calculates possible outcomes using randomly sampled values from defined probability distributions. The results show a range of possible outcomes and help decision-makers understand uncertainty. Such simulations are useful when estimates depend on several uncertain variables.

Bayes' theorem provides another way to assess risk by updating the probability of an event when new evidence becomes available. For example, an organization may revise its estimate of a cybersecurity threat after receiving new intelligence or identifying suspicious activity. Developing a quantitative model involves defining the risk, gathering reliable data, identifying variables, selecting an appropriate technique and evaluating the results. However, the accuracy of the model depends on its assumptions and available information. Numerical estimates should therefore be combined with professional judgment and reviewed regularly.

Key takeawayQuantitative models clarify uncertainty, but their outputs depend on data and assumptions.
UNIT 09 BUSINESS RESILIENCE

Risk, Business Continuity and Disaster Recovery

Business continuity focuses on maintaining essential organizational activities during disruptions, while disaster recovery concentrates on restoring systems, applications and data after an incident. A Business Impact Analysis identifies critical activities and examines the consequences of interruptions. It helps organizations determine which services should be restored first and what resources are needed. Business continuity and disaster recovery planning are important when preparing for events such as cyberattacks, equipment failures, power outages and natural disasters.

Two important recovery measurements are the Recovery Time Objective (RTO) and Recovery Point Objective (RPO). RTO describes the target time for restoring a service, while RPO describes the acceptable amount of data loss measured in time. For example, an RPO of one hour means the recovery strategy should aim to limit data loss to approximately one hour of changes. Organizations can improve recovery readiness through tested backups, clear responsibilities, communication procedures and documented recovery plans. Regular exercises help identify weaknesses before a real disruption occurs.

Key takeawayPlan both how quickly services return and how much data the organization can afford to lose.
UNIT 10 RECOVERY PLANNING

Practical Applications and Issues in Disaster Recovery Implementations

Disaster recovery solutions include regular backups, off-site storage, warm standby environments, hot standby systems, cloud recovery and Disaster Recovery as a Service (DRaaS). The choice depends on the organization's budget, business priorities and required recovery times. Systems with a short RTO may require standby infrastructure or automated failover, while a short RPO may require frequent backups or continuous data replication. Faster recovery and reduced data loss can improve resilience but may also increase costs and technical complexity.

Disaster recovery implementations can face problems such as incomplete backups, failed recovery procedures, network outages and dependence on third-party providers. DRaaS can reduce the need to maintain separate recovery infrastructure, but organizations should evaluate provider reliability, service agreements, data protection and vendor lock-in. Effective recovery requires regular testing, secure and isolated backups, updated documentation and clearly assigned responsibilities. Organizations should also ensure attackers cannot easily compromise both production systems and recovery resources during a cyber incident.

Key takeawayA recovery plan is only dependable when its backups and procedures are tested.
UNIT 11 EMERGING TRENDS

Future Trends in Security and Risk Management

Security and risk management continue to evolve because of developments in artificial intelligence, cloud computing, automation, zero-trust architecture and supply-chain security. Artificial intelligence can analyze large volumes of information, identify unusual activity, prioritize alerts and assist incident response. Automation can perform repetitive tasks such as checking system configurations, collecting logs and generating notifications. These technologies can improve efficiency and allow security professionals to focus on complex investigations and strategic decisions.

However, emerging technologies also introduce risks. AI systems may generate inaccurate results, overlook threats or be manipulated by attackers. Automation can also cause problems if systems have excessive permissions or lack suitable safeguards. Organizations should combine technological improvements with human oversight, staff training, strong access controls and regular testing. Preparing for future challenges requires continuous monitoring of emerging threats and evaluating new technologies according to their benefits, limitations, costs and potential impact.

Key takeawayAdopt new technologies carefully, with governance, testing and human oversight.

UNIT 12 THE GREAT DEBATE

The Most Influential Security and Risk Management Trend

Artificial intelligence is a strong candidate for the most influential security and risk management trend over the next five years. It can improve threat detection, analyze large amounts of security data, automate repetitive activities and help teams prioritize incidents. These capabilities may enable organizations to respond more quickly to attacks and use their resources more effectively. AI can also support risk analysis by identifying patterns that may not be immediately obvious to human analysts.

Despite these advantages, AI can produce false alerts, generate inaccurate recommendations and create opportunities for new attacks. Its implementation may require substantial investment, specialist skills and effective governance. Other trends, including cloud security, zero-trust architecture and supply-chain risk management, may also have major effects. Overall, AI is likely to influence the future of security, but organizations should use it responsibly and retain human oversight. Evidence-based discussion and consideration of opposing views help decision-makers evaluate trends realistically rather than relying only on technological popularity.

Key takeawayAI has significant potential, but responsible implementation and oversight remain essential.

I have learned the importance of user participation, threat modelling, industry standards, quantitative risk assessment, business continuity and disaster recovery. These practices help organizations protect information, reduce losses and maintain essential services when unexpected events occur.
